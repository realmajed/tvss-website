Tiered Visitor Safety System

A Ranger-run loan system that matches safety equipment to each approved visitor trip on Lama Lama Country, from a printed guide near base to a two-way satellite messenger for remote trips.

Design Area(s): 2 – Eco-tourism and Visitor Experience (Project Opportunity 2.4 – Improving visitor management and safety)

Keywords: visitor safety; remote emergency communication; satellite messenger; equipment loan; trip assessment; Ranger operations; Cape York; Lama Lama Country

Proposal Summary

Visitors to Lama Lama Country check in at Ranger bases with reliable satellite internet, then travel into areas with limited or no mobile coverage, where Rangers cannot tell if a trip has run late and visitors have no dependable way to call for help (Engineers Without Borders Australia [EWB Australia], 2026). The project aims to close this gap through a three-tier equipment loan system: Rangers assess each approved trip, issue the matching tier of equipment and record when the visitor is due back. Equipment scales with exposure, so remote trips gain two-way satellite contact while near-base trips stay simple and low-cost.

Overview of Design

The Tiered Visitor Safety System achieves trip accountability and remote emergency contact through a check-out and return process run from the Ranger base (Figure 1). Rangers apply four assessment questions to each approved trip, covering approval, nearby assistance, communication and hazards, to allocate one of three tiers. The design includes the following physical components: a printed visitor guide and trip record (Tier 1); a strobe, whistle, power bank and protective pouch (added at Tier 2); and a two-way satellite messenger (added at Tier 3). Rangers use the system to account for trips, visitors carry the kit, and Yintjingga Aboriginal Corporation (YAC) sets the rules and owns the equipment.

Key Considerations

  • Community authority. YAC and the Rangers approve destinations, tier rules, monitoring arrangements and data handling before any kit is issued.
  • Sourcing. All equipment is off the shelf from Australian retailers and is delivered through Cairns; nothing is manufactured locally (BCF, n.d.; BIG W, n.d.; Whitworths Marine & Leisure, n.d.-b).
  • Maintenance. Kits are inspected, cleaned and recharged on return, one charged Tier 3 kit is held in reserve, and satellite service is suspended over the wet season (Garmin, n.d.-a).
  • Limitations. A strobe and whistle only reach people within sight or earshot, satellite messaging cannot promise a rescue time, and radio is excluded until compatibility and coverage are confirmed (Australian Communications and Media Authority [ACMA], 2025, 2026).
  • Implementation. Staged: local review over the wet season, training and a limited dry-season pilot, then a review before any expansion.
  • Validation needed. Actual visitor demand (EWB's planning estimate is fewer than 10 tourists per week; Todio, n.d.), simultaneous remote trips, messenger performance on approved routes and Ranger time per issue.

Proposed Materials and Cost Summary

  • Purchased materials: two two-way satellite messengers (Garmin inReach Messenger as the benchmark), five accessory bundles (strobe, whistle, power bank and pouch) and satellite service for one season.
  • Other materials: printed and laminated visitor guides, trip record cards, labels, and a charging and storage point at the Ranger base.
  • Labour: a four-hour training session for four Rangers before the pilot, a two-hour refresher each dry season, and Ranger time to issue and reset kits.
  • Estimated total cost: about AUD $4,230 for the first-year pilot, including Ranger training and 15% contingency, then about AUD $1,410 per year for satellite service, refresher training, replacements and Ranger time. AUD is also the local currency.

Project

Project details

Photo: EWB Australia (2026, p. 18)

Port Stewart (Yintjingga), on Princess Charlotte Bay in eastern Cape York, is the hub for the Lama Lama Ranger program and tourism management (EWB Australia, 2026; Yintjingga Aboriginal Corporation [YAC], n.d.-a). Lama Lama National Park is not a conventional national park: Traditional Owners hold freehold title and co-manage it with Queensland Parks and Wildlife Service (EWB Australia, 2026, p. 32).

The Chair of YAC sees tourism as the community's most promising growth opportunity, with visitor fees helping to fund caring for Country (EWB Australia, 2026, p. 31). Growth that outpaces community capacity or bypasses Traditional Owner control can damage the values tourism depends on (Matson, 2020), so any visitor system must sit under Lama Lama authority.

The project responds to Design Area 2, Eco-tourism and Visitor Experience, and Project Opportunity 2.4: systems that improve visitor management and safety in a remote park, such as monitoring visitor locations and responding to emergencies where connectivity is limited (EWB Australia, 2026, p. 32).

Needs statement

Lama Lama Rangers need a simple, affordable way to account for every approved visitor trip and to receive a call for help from remote areas, because visitors travel beyond mobile coverage and the Rangers' radio network.

Project

Background

Photo: EWB Australia (2026, p. 21)
On this page

    Visiting Lama Lama Country

    Fishing and camping are the main drawcards, with campgrounds at Safari Camp (Warokutal) and Silver Plains Station (Maramba). Camping costs $15 per person per night, booked through the Cairns office or paid in cash at Running Creek, and the lack of EFTPOS limits revenue (EWB Australia, 2026, p. 31). Visitation stops in the wet season, from mid-November to mid-April, when tracks flood and the park closes (EWB Australia, 2026, pp. 27, 31). Visitor numbers are not measured; EWB's planning estimate is fewer than 10 tourists per week (Todio, n.d.).

    How communication works today

    Ranger bases and outstations have reliable Starlink and Activ8me satellite internet for reporting, coordination and calls (EWB Australia, 2026, pp. 18–21, 37). In the field, Ranger vehicles rely on VHF radio for real-time communication and emergency response (EWB Australia, 2026, p. 38). Mobile coverage is limited or non-existent in remote areas, and the pier used by fishers has no communications at all (EWB Australia, 2026, pp. 29, 37). The network reaches Rangers and buildings, but not visitors once they leave a base (Figure 5).

    When something goes wrong

    Aunty Mary, a Lama Lama Elder and former nurse, is the volunteer community health contact who coordinates Royal Flying Doctor Service evacuations (EWB Australia, 2026, p. 23). A new airstrip near the Ranger Base supports evacuations (EWB Australia, 2026, p. 29), but help still depends on someone raising the alarm. In remote Australia, retrieval can take 12 hours or more, and injury mortality is up to five times higher than in cities (Dobson et al., 2022).

    "Last year I had a bloke with a stinger ray barb in his hand. We had three months of rain and we couldn't get him to Coen because we didn't have the air strip then. He almost died on me."

    Aunty Mary, Lama Lama Elder (as cited in EWB Australia, 2026, p. 23)

    What is already being done

    Table 1Current visitor management measures and their gaps
    MeasureWhat it doesGap
    Check-in at a Ranger base, 8:00 am to 4:30 pmRangers brief visitors on campsites, conditions and rules (YAC, n.d.-b)Ends at the gate; nothing shows when a trip is overdue
    Permits and restricted areasCamping only in designated areas; Pelican, Marpa, Burkitt and Stainer Islands are closed to visitors (YAC, n.d.-b)Relies on visitors remembering the briefing

    Why this is not enough

    Each existing measure works at the base or at a sign; none follows the visitor. Australian land managers describe a growing group of less experienced, social-media-driven visitors who are less influenced by traditional safety messages (Cornell et al., 2024), and who may not carry their own beacon. The project therefore aims to give every approved trip a due-back time that someone watches, and every remote trip a way to call for help, without building infrastructure that must survive the wet season.

    Project

    Problem and design criteria

    Photo: EWB Australia (2026, p. 31)

    User needs statement

    Lama Lama Rangers need a simple, affordable way to account for every approved visitor trip and to receive a call for help from remote areas, because visitors travel beyond mobile coverage and the Rangers' radio network.

    Problem description

    Once visitors leave a Ranger base, nobody knows if a trip is overdue, and the visitor cannot reliably raise an alarm. Giving every party a satellite device would solve contact but create an affordability and workload problem for a small Ranger team with seasonal funding (EWB Australia, 2026, p. 37). The ideal situation is one where every approved trip has a due-back time someone monitors, remote trips carry two-way satellite contact, and Rangers can run, store and restart the system around the wet season without specialist help (EWB Australia, 2026, p. 27).

    Design criteria

    Each option must first pass a community-control gate: YAC approves destinations, tier rules and data handling, because Traditional Owners govern the land and its visitor access (EWB Australia, 2026, p. 32). Options that pass are then scored against five weighted criteria (Table 2).

    Design

    Design options

    Three distinct options were developed for the same problem, each adapting an existing approach to Lama Lama Country.

    Option 1

    Satellite kit for every party

    Every visiting party borrows a two-way satellite messenger and accessory kit at check-in. This extends the advice to carry a distress beacon in remote areas (Australian Maritime Safety Authority [AMSA], n.d.; DETSI, 2026), using a two-way device so Rangers can also send updates (Garmin, 2025).

    Strength
    Remote contact for every party.
    Limitation
    Highest equipment and subscription cost; the most devices to charge, track and suspend.
    Option 2

    Trip card and return register

    Each party receives a printed card with their route and due-back time, and Rangers keep a departure and return register, following up anyone overdue. This formalises standard advice to tell someone where you are going and when you will return (DETSI, 2026).

    Strength
    Simple and inexpensive.
    Limitation
    No independent remote emergency alert.
    Option 3Selected

    Three equipment tiers

    Rangers assess each trip and issue one of three tiers, so equipment follows the trip's exposure (Figure 1). Tier 1 is Option 2's card and register; Tier 3 adds Option 1's satellite messenger only where it is needed.

    Strength
    Capability follows assessed need.
    Limitation
    Needs clear tier boundaries and reliable monitoring.
    How the options evolved from earlier concepts

    Earlier in the project the team screened three different concepts: an offline visitor information and check-in app, a satellite communicator loan kit, and a campsite information station. The satellite kit scored highest but raised cost concerns, the app could not carry a call for help out of the park, and the station could not reach visitors who had already left the campground (Table 3). This feedback led to the loan-based options above. The idea map and the earlier kit concept from that presentation are in Appendix F.

    Design selection

    The options were compared in a weighted decision matrix using the criteria in Table 2. All three pass the community-control gate because each runs through YAC-approved rules.     Why each rating was given

    • Remote emergency contact. Option 1 scores 5 because every party can send an SOS. Option 3 scores 4: remote trips carry satellite contact, while other trips rely on a credible assistance arrangement. Option 2 scores 1 because an alarm is only raised once a return is missed.
    • Lifecycle affordability. Option 2 scores 5. Option 3 scores 4 because the pilot needs only two messengers and five accessory bundles (Table 10). Option 1 scores 1 because every concurrent party needs a messenger and subscription.
    • Ranger workload. Option 2 scores 4. Option 3 scores 3 because it adds a short assessment step. Option 1 scores 2 because it has the most devices to charge, track and suspend.
    • Risk matching. Option 3 scores 5 because equipment follows the assessed trip. Option 1 scores 2 and Option 2 scores 1 because each gives every trip the same response.
    • Seasonal maintainability. Option 2 scores 5, Option 3 scores 4 and Option 1 scores 2, in line with the number of electronic devices and service plans each must suspend and restart.
    Selected design

    Option 3, the three-tier system, scored highest (3.95 out of 5). It keeps Option 2's low cost for near-base trips and Option 1's satellite contact where it matters, so it was developed in detail.

    Design

    Detailed design

    On this page

      How it works

      Every approved trip passes through the same four stages (Figure 7), so Rangers learn one procedure for all three tiers.

      Trip assessment and tier allocation

      Rangers ask the same questions about every requested trip (Figure 8), and the answers set the tier (Table 5). Hazards and trip length can raise the tier. These rules are a proposal for YAC and the Rangers to review and adjust.

      Table 5The three-tier scope
      TierProposed trip conditionsEquipment
      Tier 1 · Near baseApproved, monitored activity with help readily reachablePrinted visitor guide; trip record (departure and return)
      Tier 2 · Away from baseApproved trip with a credible existing assistance arrangementTier 1 plus strobe, whistle, power bank and pouch
      Tier 3 · Remote or unmonitoredNo reliable terrestrial contact, or a long delay before helpTier 2 plus a two-way satellite messenger

      What each item does

      Table 6Equipment purpose and limits
      ItemPurposeImportant limit
      Printed guideAccess rules, hazards and agreed emergency stepsMust be updated regularly
      WhistleAudible signal to people nearbyNo long-distance distress signal
      StrobeVisual signal for locating a personNeeds line of sight and working batteries (ACR Electronics, 2016)
      Power bankRecharges compatible equipmentLimited capacity; kept for emergency charging
      Satellite messenger (Tier 3)Two-way messages, check-ins and SOS with positionNeeds open sky and an active service plan (Garmin, 2025)

      Tier 3: the two-way satellite messenger

      The Garmin inReach Messenger is the procurement benchmark (Figure 9). Two-way messaging lets a visitor send their position and situation and receive a reply, rather than only triggering an alarm. Check-ins and SOS run on the device, so no phone is needed (Garmin, 2025). Any equivalent device YAC approves could be used.

      Why radio is not included

      Ranger vehicles use VHF radio, but the radios visitors can use without an individual licence are UHF CB, and the two bands do not interoperate (ACMA, 2025, 2026; EWB Australia, 2026, p. 38). Radio should only be added once YAC confirms permissions, a monitored receiver and two-way coverage along approved routes.

      Reserve, maintenance and trip data

      One complete Tier 3 kit is kept charged as a reserve, and faulty equipment is quarantined until checked. Issuing the reserve in busy periods removes the ready replacement, and no reserve can fix a failure already in the field. Trip records hold personal information, so access is restricted and a retention period is agreed with YAC (Office of the Australian Information Commissioner [OAIC], 2018).

      Community concerns and responses

      Will this add to the Rangers' workload?
      Issue and reset follow a one-page procedure, and the pilot measures the real time per issue before any expansion.
      Who decides where visitors can go and which tier they get?
      YAC and the Rangers. The tier rules here are a starting point for local review.
      Does logging trips intrude on privacy or cultural knowledge?
      Only the route, due-back time and kit ID are recorded, and access is restricted (OAIC, 2018). The guide shows restricted boundaries without describing cultural sites.
      What happens if a kit is lost or damaged?
      Faulty kits are swapped for the reserve. Any bond or charge needs YAC approval and an affordability assessment.
      Does this replace the Ranger briefing?
      No. The kit and the teach-back step strengthen the briefing.

      Prototype

      Prototyping and testing

      What was prototyped and why

      The devices are proven products, so the riskiest part of the design is the handover: whether visitors understand the guide after a short briefing, whether issue and reset fit a Ranger's workload, and whether different Rangers give the same trip the same tier. A low-cost physical prototype was built to test these three questions.

      Delivery

      Implementation plan

      Photo: EWB Australia (2026, p. 18)

      Implementation is staged around the seasons. Planning happens during the wet season when visitation stops, equipment arrives once roads reopen in mid-April, and the pilot runs through one full dry season (Figure 12; EWB Australia, 2026, p. 27). Local approval and equipment tests come before any visitor is issued a kit.

      Who does what

      Table 8Roles and responsibilities
      WhoRole in the system
      Yintjingga Aboriginal CorporationApproves destinations, tier rules and data handling; owns the equipment and records; decides on charges and expansion
      Lama Lama RangersAssess trips, issue and reset kits, monitor due returns and follow up overdue trips
      VisitorsCarry the kit, follow the guide, check in and return on time
      Community health contact and RFDSExisting emergency response and evacuation (EWB Australia, 2026, pp. 23, 29; Royal Flying Doctor Service [RFDS], n.d.)
      SuppliersSatellite service and equipment warranty (Garmin, n.d.-a; BCF, n.d.)

      Training

      Rangers learn the system from a one-page procedure and a hands-on demonstration, then practise the teach-back briefing. A short refresher at the start of each dry season matters because funding cycles mean Ranger teams change between seasons (EWB Australia, 2026, p. 37). The first session (about four hours for four Rangers) and each two-hour refresher are costed in Tables 10 and 11.

      Evaluation

      The pilot records issue and reset time, teach-back pass rate, overdue returns and follow-ups, equipment faults, messages and SOS events, and visitor feedback. YAC reviews these at the end of the season before deciding on quantities or expansion.

      Ownership and repair

      YAC owns the equipment and the records, and the Rangers can change the tier rules and guide as they learn. When something fails, the kit is quarantined and swapped for the reserve, warranty claims go through the retailer, and consumables such as batteries are replaced from spares held at base.

      Delivery

      Recommendations

      The following work sits outside this project's scope but is needed for the system to succeed:

      • Record visitor numbers, party sizes and trip lengths for a full season to size the kit pool (Todio, n.d.).
      • Field-test messenger performance along each approved route, including under tree cover and on the water.
      • Confirm radio compatibility, licensing and a monitored receiver before adding any radio (ACMA, 2025, 2026).
      • Co-design the visitor guide with the Rangers, including language and cultural content approved by YAC.
      • Link with Opportunity 4.2, Ranger communications, so visitor and Ranger systems share devices, plans and procedures (EWB Australia, 2026, p. 38).
      • Assess Indigenous Rangers Program eligibility for ongoing costs (NIAA, n.d.).
